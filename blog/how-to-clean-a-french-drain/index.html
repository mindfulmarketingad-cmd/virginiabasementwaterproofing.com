How To Clean A French Drain

Published July 30, 2026  ·  10 min read

Most of what I've written about French drains covers building one. This article covers the other end of the timeline: what to do with one that already exists and has started acting up. A French drain that's draining slower than it used to, or has stopped draining at all, isn't necessarily dead — but figuring out whether it's cleanable or needs to be rebuilt depends on understanding exactly what's clogged, which isn't always obvious from the surface. If you're not sure whether the problem you're seeing is even a French drain issue, how to waterproof a basement is a good place to confirm the diagnosis first.

Signs Your French Drain Needs Attention

  • Water pooling along the trench line after rain, where it used to drain away within a few hours.
  • Reduced or no flow at the discharge point during a storm that would normally produce visible water there.
  • A soggy area returning that the drain had previously solved.
  • A gurgling sound from the discharge point or a nearby cleanout, which can indicate a partial blockage trapping air.
  • Settling or a visible depression along the trench line, which can mean the gravel has compacted or washed out beneath the surface.

Any one of these on its own isn't necessarily an emergency, but seeing two or more together, especially after several years of otherwise normal performance, is a reasonably strong sign it's time to actually inspect the system rather than wait it out.

Figure Out What's Actually Clogged First

This is the step that determines everything else, and it's the one I see skipped most often by homeowners who jump straight to renting a jetter. There are really two different failure modes, and they need completely different fixes:

  • The pipe itself is blocked — by sediment that made it inside, by tree roots that have grown in through a joint or perforation seeking moisture, or by a physical obstruction. This is a plumbing problem, and it's often fixable without digging anything up.
  • The gravel around the pipe has silted up — fine soil particles have worked past or around the filter fabric over the years and filled in the open pore space the whole system depends on. The pipe itself can be completely clear and the drain will still perform poorly, because water can no longer travel through the clogged gravel to reach it. This is not a plumbing problem, and no amount of flushing or jetting the pipe will fix it — the affected section of gravel has to be excavated and replaced.

I go through exactly why the gravel's pore space matters this much for the whole system in how a French drain actually works, which is worth reading if you want to understand why this distinction is as important as it is.

How to Tell Which One You're Dealing With

If you have access to a cleanout or can expose an end of the pipe, a plumbing snake or a garden hose pushed into the pipe will tell you a lot: if you feel resistance a specific distance in, or water backs up at a predictable point, that points toward a pipe obstruction. If the pipe itself seems clear when you can access it, but the drain still performs poorly across its whole length, that points toward the gravel having silted up instead — a problem no amount of pipe-clearing will solve. A camera inspection, which most drainage and plumbing contractors offer as a service, removes the guesswork entirely by actually showing you the inside of the pipe.

Cleaning Methods, From Simplest to Most Involved

  • Hose flush. If you can access an end of the pipe or a cleanout, running a garden hose into it at moderate pressure can clear light sediment buildup and confirm whether water is moving freely. This is the simplest DIY starting point and costs nothing but time.
  • Plumbing snake / auger. For a specific, localized blockage — a root intrusion or a lodged obstruction — a drain snake fed through a cleanout can physically break through and clear it. This is a reasonable DIY attempt if you have cleanout access, though awkward without one.
  • Hydro-jetting. A high-pressure water jet fed through the pipe that scours the interior far more thoroughly than a snake, effective against built-up sediment and root intrusion along the full length of a run. This typically requires rented or professional equipment most homeowners don't keep on hand, and it only addresses the pipe itself — not clogged gravel around it.
  • Clearing the discharge point. Don't overlook the simplest possible cause: leaves, mulch, soil creep, or a collapsed pop-up emitter at the discharge end can block flow just as effectively as anything clogging the pipe itself, and it costs nothing to check and clear first.

When Cleaning Won't Fix It

If you've confirmed the pipe itself is clear — by camera, by successfully snaking or jetting it, or simply by exposing and checking an accessible section — and the drain is still performing poorly, the problem is almost certainly the gravel, not the pipe. At that point, the only real fix is excavating the affected section, removing the silted gravel, and rebuilding it with clean stone and proper filter fabric, exactly as described in how to install a French drain. This doesn't necessarily mean redoing the entire run — often just the section showing the worst performance needs to be rebuilt, particularly if the rest of the trench is still working reasonably well.

Preventing the Problem in the First Place

  • Keep trees and large shrubs away from the trench line. Roots seek out moisture and will grow toward and into a French drain given the chance, which is one of the most common causes of pipe blockage over time.
  • Check the discharge point seasonally, especially after leaves fall or after a significant storm, since debris collects there more than almost anywhere else in the system.
  • Do an annual flush if you have cleanout access, rather than waiting for a problem to appear. A few minutes with a garden hose once a year is far cheaper than re-excavating a silted section later.
  • Make sure downspouts and surface grading haven't shifted to dump additional silt-laden runoff directly into the drain's catch area, which accelerates gravel clogging.

Tools Worth Having on Hand

  • A garden hose for the basic flush test, and for checking flow at the discharge point.
  • A plumbing snake or hand auger if you have cleanout access and suspect a localized blockage.
  • A flashlight or headlamp for actually looking inside a cleanout or an exposed pipe end rather than working blind.
  • Work gloves — standing water and silt in an older drain isn't something you want directly on your hands.
  • A small shop vacuum if you're pulling debris out of a cleanout access point rather than just flushing it through.

None of this requires specialized equipment for the basic checks; the jump to rented or professional equipment only really happens once you've confirmed the pipe itself needs jetting or a camera inspection.

What This Costs

A basic hose flush costs nothing but your own time. Professional hydro-jetting or camera inspection commonly runs a few hundred dollars depending on the length of the run and access. Rebuilding a silted gravel section is closer to the cost of a fresh installation for that portion — often $2,000 to $6,500 for a full exterior run, prorated down for a shorter section. When I'd Call a Professional Instead of Troubleshooting It Myself

I'd handle a hose flush and a visual check of the discharge point myself without a second thought. I'd call in a professional for hydro-jetting, camera inspection, or any situation where I couldn't clearly tell whether the problem was the pipe or the gravel around it — guessing wrong there means paying for jetting that was never going to fix a silted gravel bed. Conclusion

Cleaning a French drain starts with correctly diagnosing what's actually clogged, because a blocked pipe and silted gravel look similar from the surface but need completely different fixes — one is a flush or a snake away from solved, the other needs excavation no amount of jetting will substitute for. Catch the early signs, keep tree roots away from the line, and do a simple annual flush, and most French drains never reach the point of needing a rebuild at all.
